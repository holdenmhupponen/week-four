# Connections

I think the Google Colab project helped to experience the idea of "maps as performances" as Haciguzeller explains. From what I gathered from _Archaeological (Digital Maps) as Performances_, he believes that modern tools in digital archaeology have created a performative angle in cartography, which encourages cooperative and ongoing work. Beforehand, the limits of technology meant that cartographers were a specific group of few people, who had to fund their own travels and create maps with expensive paper and pen. The cost of the human experience and labour as well as the materials required the staticity of cartography: Unlike today, you required existential resources if you wanted to check or improve upon an existing map. Today, though, Haciguzeller thinks that digital software has dispersed this knowledge to the masses. Digital servers can be accessed from all around the world. The demanded trust that these maps are correct can be challenged by anyone with a working internet connection. Especially because, as he writes in _Archaeology, Crafting Maps and Political Change_, we have come to understand the deep political connotations that maps embody. With the destruction of that belief in the "perfect objectivity" of maps by digital archaeology, more perspectives from more people can be heard. In my Google Colab experience, I have personally seen both a) how easy it has become for even a layman (like myself) to participate in cartography, and b) how unbiased the role of the cartographer really is. Firstly, I did not have to map anything out myself or even write a line of code. The values were already inputted, and because of digital software, I could access them even thousands of kilometers away. Secondly, I had an immense amount of power in how I represented the data. I could play around with variables. I could isolate different parts that I was interested in or wanted to investigate. I could have deliberately changed some values to misrepresent what the data actually presented. I hadn't had personal experience with cartography before, so I hadn't personally realized the extent that bias plays into the creation of a map. I agree with Haciguzeller's idea that performative maps have exposed the political veils of cartography: Because if I were to have, say, intentionally misrepresented data on my Log.md file, my professor or one of my teaching assistants could have quickly checked the Google Colab values and seen how the data actually existed. Digital tools allow that process of peer review to flourish beyond what used to be capable.
